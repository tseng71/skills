# Narrative and visual grammar

This synthesis is based on close reading of 11 Pudding stories across 2022–2025, including their live openings, scroll states, controls, exploratory sections, methods, and endings. Use it to design the story's logic, not to imitate The Pudding's brand.

## 1. The recurring narrative engine

Strong Pudding stories repeatedly use this progression:

1. **Recognition** — begin with a person, object, ritual, choice, or question already legible to the reader.
2. **Prediction** — let the reader form an expectation, sometimes through a choice or micro-interaction.
3. **Complication** — reveal the first result that contradicts or sharpens that expectation.
4. **Teach the visual** — introduce one encoding at a time using a concrete example.
5. **Expand the field** — preserve the example while adding the population, timeline, or comparison set.
6. **Explain the mechanism** — rearrange the same marks, run a simulation, or expose the rules that produced the pattern.
7. **Hand over agency** — only after the reader understands the grammar, allow search, filters, sliders, scrubbing, or open exploration.
8. **Return** — revisit the opening example or question with new meaning.

This is not a mandatory eight-part outline. It is a cognitive sequence: concrete before abstract, prediction before answer, explanation before exploration, and return before exit.

## 2. The visual state ladder

Storyboard the central visual as a sequence of semantic operations. Prefer reusing the same entities over replacing one complete chart with another.

| Operation | Reader learns | Typical visual move |
|---|---|---|
| Instantiate | “This is the thing we are following.” | one bottle, person, animal, onion, melody, or place appears |
| Demonstrate | “This is how it behaves.” | one cut, transaction, conversation, or policy obstacle plays out |
| Duplicate | “This is not a singular case.” | more instances enter while the first remains identifiable |
| Align | “These can be compared.” | instances share a baseline, timeline, map, or coordinate system |
| Encode | “This property differs.” | position, size, color, shape, line, or texture gains a named meaning |
| Reorder | “A structure is emerging.” | sort, cluster, split, rank, or group the same marks |
| Annotate | “Notice this relationship.” | direct labels, thresholds, examples, or a highlighted path appear |
| Aggregate | “The individual cases form a pattern.” | distribution, median, trend, quadrant, or cumulative measure appears |
| Mechanize | “This is why it happens.” | simulation, rule, path, or cause-and-effect transition is exposed |
| Explore | “Now test another case or assumption.” | search, filters, sliders, scrubbing, or free play becomes available |
| Return | “The opening now means something different.” | original entity or question reappears inside the full context |

For every transition, name the operation and the claim it carries. If the only answer is “it looks more dramatic,” remove it.

## 3. Story-specific visual language

The strongest projects do not look like a generic publication template. They derive their visual material from the subject:

- onion layers and cut pieces become both illustration and measurement;
- children’s-book animal stickers become statistical marks;
- legal and administrative barriers become maze walls and routes;
- people remain recognizable silhouettes while their lives unfold across decades;
- wine bottles begin as consumer objects before becoming dots in a value space;
- pitch contours let readers see and hear melodic deviation;
- minutes of a day become the physical pacing of a loneliness story.

Before selecting chart types, define:

- **visual noun:** the persistent thing the reader follows;
- **visual verb:** what it does across the story—moves, splits, accumulates, collides, ages, deviates, disappears;
- **comparison frame:** the stable baseline, timeline, map, or coordinate system;
- **annotation voice:** how the story points without turning into a legend-heavy dashboard;
- **subject texture:** imagery, symbols, sounds, or motion native to the topic;
- **ethical boundary:** what must not be gamified, aestheticized, or made anonymous.

Reject a visual system that could be reused unchanged for an unrelated dataset by replacing labels.

## 4. How charts enter the story

Do not drop a finished chart into the page and explain it afterward. Teach the chart through the sequence.

Use this order when complexity warrants it:

1. show a recognizable object or example;
2. demonstrate the measured property;
3. place the example on one axis or timeline;
4. introduce comparison cases;
5. name the second encoding or threshold;
6. reveal the full distribution;
7. annotate the central finding;
8. expose controls for alternative definitions or examples.

The reader should know what a mark represents before seeing hundreds of marks. Axes and denominators should be introduced at the moment they become meaningful. Prefer direct labels and highlighted examples over detached legends.

Complex chart forms are earned when the reader has already watched them being assembled. This allows a scatterplot, heatmap, or dense matrix to feel like the inevitable next sentence rather than a technical interruption.

## 5. Five experience structures

### A. Guided visual argument

Use when the order of evidence matters. Scroll advances discrete claims while a persistent graphic changes state. End with an optional explorer.

### B. Continuous lived time

Use when duration is itself evidence. Map scroll distance to a real temporal sequence only when the pacing helps the reader feel accumulation, waiting, repetition, or absence. Insert authored checkpoints so time does not become an empty scroll gimmick.

### C. Choice-first investigation

Use when the reader has a natural prior belief. Open with a small consequential choice, preserve it, and later show whether the data supports it. Always allow skipping.

### D. Search-first atlas

Use when readers naturally want to find a place, name, item, or identity. Provide curated examples, a useful default, an opinionated explanation of the model, and an authored path beyond the lookup.

### E. Playable mechanism

Use when the rules themselves are the argument. The interaction should reproduce a real constraint, feedback loop, or trade-off. Pair play with a non-playable explanation and state where the model departs from reality.

## 6. Interaction and reader agency

Separate three kinds of agency:

- **narrative agency:** choose a path, person, or example without losing the thesis;
- **analytical agency:** change a real definition, threshold, subset, or assumption;
- **participatory agency:** contribute data or reflection to an experiment.

Introduce agency only where it answers a question the reader is likely to have. A well-authored default must carry the story for readers who never interact.

Use the “teach, then hand over” rule:

1. demonstrate the control's dimension in the authored narrative;
2. show a meaningful preset;
3. give the reader control;
4. preserve the global context and the authored takeaway;
5. provide reset, empty, and out-of-range states.

Do not use interaction as a locked door between paragraphs. Do not demand play on sensitive topics. Provide observer, skip, pause, or all-text modes where appropriate.

## 7. Pacing, copy, and annotation

Pudding stories often alternate between large experiential scenes and compact explanatory passages. Use pacing deliberately:

- open with less interface and more attention;
- keep early copy short enough for the visual object to register;
- slow down for the first non-obvious encoding;
- accelerate through repeated comparisons once the grammar is learned;
- pause before the central reveal;
- allow denser text in mechanism and methods sections;
- reduce interaction density near the ending.

Write copy that advances the argument, not copy that inventories the screen. Prefer “The pieces near the bottom are larger” to “This chart shows piece sizes.” Let labels sit on or next to the marks they explain.

Use humor, informality, or first person when it comes from the authorial voice and subject. Do not paste a generic “Pudding tone” onto grief, health, violence, or inequality.

## 8. Endings

A strong ending is not a dashboard, a generic summary, or “explore the data.” It does at least two of:

- returns to the opening person, object, choice, or scene;
- states how the reader's initial model changed;
- distinguishes observed evidence from modeled explanation;
- acknowledges what the data cannot decide;
- restores human stakes after abstraction;
- offers a concrete next question, practice, or way of seeing.

Exploration may remain available after the authored ending, but it should not replace resolution.

## 9. Art-direction rules

Create a subject-specific art direction system before implementation:

- one dominant visual material and one supporting material;
- a restrained palette with an explicit data role for every analytical color;
- typography that supports tone without imitating The Pudding's house identity;
- recurring shapes or illustrations that can survive both narrative and chart states;
- deliberate negative space at openings and reveals;
- direct, compact annotations;
- a mobile composition that is redesigned, not merely shrunk.

Distinctive does not mean busy. Many strong openings use a single object, a short sentence, and generous empty space.

## 10. Failure modes

Reject or revise:

- a homepage that explains the internal product brief instead of inviting the reader into a world;
- a sequence of unrelated chart types connected only by scrolling;
- a “creative” metaphor that cannot preserve quantitative meaning;
- illustrations that disappear when the data begins;
- a full explorer before the reader knows what to look for;
- controls whose effect is decorative or unclear;
- a map used only because geographic fields exist;
- one full-screen visual treatment repeated at the same intensity for the entire story;
- motion without a named semantic operation;
- desktop art direction squeezed into mobile;
- an ending that abandons the opening case;
- a visual identity copied from The Pudding instead of derived from the subject.

## 11. Design review checklist

Before approving a storyboard, answer:

- What is the opening recognition object?
- What prediction or expectation does the reader form?
- What is the central visual noun and visual verb?
- Which entity persists from concrete example to population?
- How is the first encoding taught?
- What exact state produces the central reveal?
- Which transition explains mechanism rather than merely changing scenes?
- When does authored guidance become exploration?
- What does a non-interacting reader still understand?
- What changes on mobile besides size?
- How does reduced motion preserve the explanation?
- How does the ending return to the opening?
- Could this visual system belong only to this subject?
